# Task Statement 4.1 — Generative AI Challenges, Risks, and Responsible AI Controls

## 🎯 Exam Essentials

### **1. Generative AI Hallucinations**

- **Concept:** A hallucination occurs when a generative AI model produces information that sounds factual but is actually fictional.
    
- **Key distinction:** The output can be convincing even though the underlying information is incorrect.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Plausible-sounding but fabricated information → hallucination.**
    

---

### **2. Why Hallucinations Occur**

- **Concept:** The transcript describes hallucinations as occurring when the model attempts to fill gaps when information is missing from its training data.
    
- **Key distinction:** A confident-sounding response does not guarantee factual correctness.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Missing information + fabricated completion → hallucination risk.**
    

---

### **3. Impact of Hallucinations**

- **Concept:** Hallucinations can cause serious real-world consequences depending on how generated content is used.
    
- **Key distinction:** The risk is not merely that the output is inaccurate; it can affect legal, financial, medical, or other consequential decisions.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Generated information used for consequential decisions → hallucination becomes a serious risk.**
    

---

### **4. Intellectual Property Risk**

- **Concept:** Generative AI can create IP-related risks because training data may contain copyrighted, patented, or trademarked material.
    
- **Key distinction:** Protected material in training data may potentially influence generated outputs.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Copyright/patent/trademark material in training data → IP risk.**
    

---

### **5. User-Provided Copyrighted Content**

- **Concept:** A user can provide copyrighted material as a prompt/input.
    
- **Key distinction:** The model may then generate an unlicensed derivative of that material.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Copyrighted input → potential unlicensed derivative output.**
    

---

### **6. Biased Outputs**

- **Concept:** Biased model outputs can result in discriminatory or unfair treatment of individuals or groups.
    
- **Key distinction:** Bias is not merely a technical issue; it can create **legal and societal risks**.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Biased output causing unequal treatment → discrimination/legal risk.**
    

---

### **7. Toxic and Offensive Content**

- **Concept:** Generative AI can generate offensive, disturbing, obscene, hateful, aggressive, or otherwise toxic content if such content exists in its training data.
    
- **Key distinction:** The model can reproduce harmful patterns present in its training data.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Harmful content generation → toxicity/content-safety risk.**
    

---

### **8. Real-World Harm From Toxic Content**

- **Concept:** Toxic generated content can cause real-world harm, including mental/emotional harm and potentially increased propensity toward violence against individuals or marginalized groups.
    
- **Key distinction:** Responsible AI must consider downstream effects, not just whether the generated text is technically valid.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Offensive/toxic output + potential user harm → responsible AI risk.**
    

---

### **9. Data Privacy Risk**

- **Concept:** Sensitive information that enters an LLM can potentially leak into generated outputs.
    
- **Key distinction:** Sensitive data can enter the model through **training data or user prompts**.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **PII/IP/trade secrets/healthcare records + LLM → data privacy risk.**
    

---

### **10. Types of Sensitive Information at Risk**

- **Concept:** The transcript specifically identifies:
    
    - Personally identifiable information (PII)
        
    - Intellectual property
        
    - Trade secrets
        
    - Healthcare records
        
- **Key distinction:** Both organizational and personal sensitive information can be exposed.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Sensitive information in training data or prompts → potential leakage risk.**
    

---

### **11. Difficulty of Removing Learned Information**

- **Concept:** The transcript states that once a foundation model has been trained on or exposed to data, deleting the original data does not make the model automatically forget it.
    
- **Key distinction:** **Deleting the source data ≠ automatically removing the model's learned information.**
    
- **Exam trigger:** **"Delete the dataset to make the model forget" → not sufficient according to the transcript.**
    

---

### **12. Loss of Trust and Reputation**

- **Concept:** Hallucinations, IP issues, bias, toxicity, and privacy failures can result in loss of customer trust and reputational damage.
    
- **Key distinction:** Responsible AI failures can have business consequences beyond the model itself.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Multiple AI risks → customer trust + reputation risk.**
    

---

### **13. Amazon Bedrock Guardrails**

- **Concept:** Bedrock Guardrails can filter and block inappropriate content.
    
- **Key distinction:** Guardrails can be configured with thresholds for different categories and can also block entire topics.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Amazon Bedrock + inappropriate content filtering → Guardrails.**
    

---

### **14. Content Filter Thresholds**

- **Concept:** Guardrails allow thresholds to be configured for categories such as **hate, insults, sexual content, and violence**.
    
- **Key distinction:** The organization can control how strictly content categories are filtered.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Need configurable content-safety thresholds → Bedrock Guardrails.**
    

---

### **15. Topic Blocking**

- **Concept:** Bedrock Guardrails can block specified topics altogether.
    
- **Key distinction:** Topic blocking differs from simply setting a content-filter threshold for generated content.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Entire topic must be denied → configure denied topics in Guardrails.**
    

---

### **16. Guardrails Inspect User Prompts**

- **Concept:** A user prompt passes through the guardrail before reaching the model.
    
- **Key distinction:** If the prompt violates the configured policy, it **does not reach the model**.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Disallowed prompt → Guardrail blocks it before model invocation.**
    

---

### **17. Guardrails Inspect Model Responses**

- **Concept:** Guardrails can also evaluate the model's generated response.
    
- **Key distinction:** Even when a prompt passes the guardrail, the **generated response can still be blocked**.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Prompt allowed ≠ response automatically allowed.**
    

---

### **18. Configured Violation Response**

- **Concept:** When a prompt is rejected by a guardrail, the user receives a configured violation response.
    
- **Key distinction:** The blocked prompt never reaches the model.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Guardrail violation → configured violation response.**
    

---

### **19. SageMaker Clarify LLM Evaluation**

- **Concept:** SageMaker Clarify can run evaluation jobs for large language models and compare models.
    
- **Key distinction:** The transcript describes four task types and five evaluation dimensions.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **AWS evaluation of LLM behavior across multiple dimensions → SageMaker Clarify evaluation jobs.**
    

---

### **20. Clarify LLM Evaluation Task Types**

- **Concept:** The transcript lists four evaluation task types:
    
    1. Text generation
        
    2. Text classification
        
    3. Question answering
        
    4. Text summarization
        
- **Key distinction:** These represent different ways of evaluating LLM behavior.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Clarify LLM evaluation → remember the four task categories.**
    

---

### **21. Prompt Stereotyping**

- **Concept:** Prompt stereotyping measures the probability that a model includes biases in its response.
    
- **Key distinction:** The transcript identifies bias dimensions including race, gender, sexual orientation, religion, age, nationality, disability, physical appearance, and socioeconomic status.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **LLM response contains demographic/social stereotypes → prompt stereotyping.**
    

---

### **22. Toxicity Evaluation**

- **Concept:** Toxicity evaluation checks whether model responses contain harmful behaviors or content.
    
- **Key distinction:** The transcript includes sexual references, rude/unreasonable/hateful/aggressive comments, profanity, insults, flirtations, identity attacks, and threats.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Hateful/profane/aggressive/insulting output → toxicity evaluation.**
    

---

### **23. Factual Knowledge Evaluation**

- **Concept:** Factual knowledge evaluation checks the **veracity** of model responses.
    
- **Key distinction:** It focuses on whether generated information is factually correct.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Is the generated answer true? → factual knowledge.**
    

---

### **24. Semantic Robustness**

- **Concept:** Semantic robustness checks whether model output changes when the input is modified in small, meaning-preserving ways.
    
- **Key distinction:** The transcript gives examples such as keyword typos, random capitalization changes, and random additions/deletions of whitespace.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Minor input perturbation → significantly different output → semantic robustness concern.**
    

---

### **25. Accuracy Evaluation**

- **Concept:** Accuracy compares model output with expected responses.
    
- **Key distinction:** The transcript gives classification and summarization as examples of tasks where correctness can be compared against expected results.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Generated answer vs expected answer → accuracy.**
    

---

### **26. Built-In vs Custom Evaluation Datasets**

- **Concept:** Clarify evaluation can use a built-in prompt dataset or a dataset supplied by the user.
    
- **Key distinction:** Organizations can evaluate against their own prompts rather than being restricted to a predefined dataset.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Custom evaluation scenarios → supply your own prompt dataset.**
    

---

### **27. Human Workforce Evaluation**

- **Concept:** Human workers such as employees or subject matter experts can provide feedback during model evaluation.
    
- **Key distinction:** Automated metrics can be complemented by human judgment.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Need human/SME feedback on model responses → human workforce evaluation.**
    

---

### **28. Amazon Bedrock Evaluation**

- **Concept:** The transcript states that equivalent evaluation capabilities are available through the Amazon Bedrock console for evaluating pretrained LLMs in Bedrock.
    
- **Key distinction:** The evaluation functionality is available within the Bedrock environment as well.
    
- **Exam trigger:** **Evaluating pretrained Bedrock LLMs → Bedrock console evaluation capabilities.**

## # 📝 Exam Questions

### **Q1.**

A company deploys a Bedrock-based application. A user submits a prompt requesting content from a topic that the organization has explicitly configured as denied. What should happen according to the described Guardrails behavior?

**A.** The prompt is sent to the model, and the response is filtered afterward.  
**B.** The prompt is blocked before reaching the model, and the configured violation response is returned.  
**C.** The model generates a response, which is then stored for human review.  
**D.** The prompt bypasses the guardrail because only model responses can be filtered.

**Answer: B**

**Why:** A violating prompt is blocked **before it reaches the model**, and the user receives the configured violation response.

---

### **Q2.**

A prompt passes an Amazon Bedrock Guardrail, but the model subsequently generates content that violates the configured content policy. What does the lesson indicate can happen?

**A.** The response can still be blocked by the guardrail.  
**B.** The response must be returned because the prompt already passed.  
**C.** The model must be retrained before the response can be filtered.  
**D.** Only human workers can prevent the response from reaching the user.

**Answer: A**

**Why:** Guardrails can be applied to **both prompts and model responses**.
