# Domain 5 — Task Statement 5.2: Recognize governance and compliance regulations for AI systems

## 🎯 Exam Essentials

### **1. Emerging AI Compliance Standards**

- **Concept:** AI-specific compliance standards are still **emerging** and are only beginning to see widespread adoption.
    
- **Key distinction:** AI governance is developing around mechanisms for assessing and managing risks associated with AI systems.
    
- **Exam trigger:** A question asks why AI compliance is still evolving → think **emerging standards and risk-management frameworks**.
    

### **2. ISO 402001 and ISO 23894**

- **Concept:** The transcript identifies **ISO 402001** and **ISO 23894**, published in 2023, as standards establishing mechanisms for assessing and managing AI-system risk.
    
- **Key distinction:** These standards provide a framework for organizations to systematically address and control risks related to developing and deploying AI.
    
- **Exam trigger:** AI risk assessment/management standards published in 2023 → recall **ISO 402001 and ISO 23894** as presented in the transcript.
    
- **⚠️ Accuracy note:** The transcript gives the standard number as **“ISO 402001.”** Per the source-fidelity rule, this is preserved rather than silently corrected or replaced with outside knowledge.
    

### **3. Responsible AI and Global Interoperability**

- **Concept:** The ISO standards described in the transcript emphasize **responsible AI practices** and controls specific to AI systems.
    
- **Key distinction:** They also aim to foster **global interoperability** and provide a foundation for responsible AI development and deployment.
    
- **Exam trigger:** A question connects AI standards with responsible AI and global interoperability → think **AI-specific controls + responsible AI + interoperability**.
    

### **4. Compliance Standards vs. Legal Requirements**

- **Concept:** Compliance standards are highly recommended but are not necessarily **legal requirements**.
    
- **Key distinction:** A voluntary or recommended standard is different from a regulation that creates legally enforceable requirements.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Distinguish **standards/frameworks** from **laws/regulations**.
    

### **5. EU AI Act**

- **Concept:** The **EU AI Act** is a European regulation concerning artificial intelligence.
    
- **Key distinction:** The transcript describes it as the first comprehensive AI regulation by a major regulator and categorizes AI applications according to risk.
    
- **Exam trigger:** European AI regulation with risk-based categories → **EU AI Act**.
    

### **6. Three EU AI Act Risk Categories**

- **Concept:** The transcript divides AI applications under the EU AI Act into three broad risk categories: **unacceptable risk, high risk, and applications not explicitly banned or listed as high risk**.
    
- **Key distinction:** The regulatory treatment becomes stricter as the identified risk increases.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Identify the category first, then determine the associated regulatory treatment.
    

### **7. Unacceptable-Risk AI Applications**

- **Concept:** AI applications considered to create **unacceptable risk** are banned outright according to the transcript.
    
- **Examples:** Social-scoring systems based on social behavior, facial-recognition databases scraping facial images from the internet, and systems that infer emotions in workplaces or educational institutions.
    
- **Exam trigger:** An AI system falls into a prohibited/unacceptable-risk category → **banned**.
    

### **8. High-Risk AI Applications**

- **Concept:** **High-risk** AI applications are subject to specific legal requirements.
    
- **Example:** A CV-scanning tool that ranks job applicants.
    
- **Key distinction:** High-risk systems are not treated the same way as outright banned applications; they are subject to defined requirements.
    
- **Exam trigger:** AI used in consequential decision-making, such as ranking job applicants → consider **high-risk requirements**.
    

### **9. Requirements for High-Risk AI**

- **Concept:** The transcript identifies requirements including establishing a **risk management system**, conducting **data governance**, and **documenting compliance**.
    
- **Key distinction:** High-risk AI requires structured risk and governance processes rather than simply deploying the model.
    
- **Exam trigger:** High-risk AI compliance requirements → **risk management + data governance + compliance documentation**.
    

### **10. Lower-Risk / Other AI Applications**

- **Concept:** Applications that are not explicitly banned or listed as high risk are described in the transcript as **largely left unregulated**.
    
- **Key distinction:** Regulatory obligations depend on the risk classification.
    
- **Exam trigger:** An AI application does not fall into the prohibited or high-risk categories → consider the transcript's **largely unregulated** category.
    

### **11. EU Regulations and Global Influence**

- **Concept:** The transcript recommends paying attention to EU regulations even when an AI system is not intended for EU citizens.
    
- **Key distinction:** Regulations can influence global practices and become de facto global standards.
    
- **Example:** The transcript uses **GDPR** as an example.
    
- **Exam trigger:** A company doesn't directly target EU citizens but wants to anticipate globally influential regulations → consider **EU regulatory influence**.
    

### **12. NIST AI Risk Management Framework**

- **Concept:** The **AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)** was released by the **National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)**.
    
- **Key distinction:** It provides guidance for trustworthy and responsible development and use of AI systems.
    
- **Exam trigger:** U.S. framework providing guidance for managing AI risks → **NIST AI RMF**.
    

### **13. Purpose of AI RMF**

- **Concept:** AI RMF is intended to help organizations that **design, develop, deploy, or use AI systems** manage AI-related risks.
    
- **Key distinction:** It addresses the lifecycle of AI use rather than focusing only on model development.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Organization wants guidance for managing AI risk throughout development/deployment/use → **AI RMF**.
    

### **14. Voluntary Nature of AI RMF**

- **Concept:** The transcript states that the AI RMF is intended to be **voluntary**.
    
- **Key distinction:** AI RMF is guidance rather than a mandatory regulation in the framing provided by the transcript.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Distinguish **NIST AI RMF** from legally binding regulations such as the EU AI Act.
    

### **15. Four AI RMF Functions**

- **Concept:** NIST AI RMF describes four functions: **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage**.
    
- **Key distinction:** These functions provide a structured approach for addressing AI-system risks.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Memorize the four functions as **G-M-M-M: Govern → Map → Measure → Manage**.
    

### **16. Risk Estimation**

- **Concept:** According to the transcript's description of NIST RMF, risk can be estimated by multiplying the **likelihood of an event** by the **severity of its consequences**.
    
- **Formula:** **Risk = Likelihood × Severity**
    
- **Exam trigger:** A question gives likelihood and consequence severity and asks how risk is estimated → **multiply them**.
    

### **17. Risk Matrix**

- **Concept:** A risk matrix can be used to classify risk based on **likelihood** and **severity**.
    
- **Example:** An event with low severity and rare likelihood can be classified as **very low risk**.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Risk classification based on two dimensions → **likelihood + severity**.
    

### **18. Identify the AI Use Case and Stakeholders**

- **Concept:** Risk assessment should begin by identifying the **AI use case** and the relevant **stakeholders**.
    
- **Key distinction:** Understanding how users will use the system to achieve a particular goal establishes the context for identifying risks.
    
- **Exam trigger:** First step in the described AI risk-estimation process → **identify the use case and relevant stakeholders**.
    

### **19. Identify Potentially Harmful Events**

- **Concept:** After establishing the use case and stakeholders, identify **potentially harmful events** associated with the use case.
    
- **Key distinction:** Each harmful event can then be evaluated using the risk matrix.
    
- **Exam trigger:** After identifying the use case → identify **potentially harmful events**.
    

### **20. Inherent Risk**

- **Concept:** **Inherent risk** is the risk associated with a system before applying the described mitigations.
    
- **Key distinction:** Proper security controls and monitoring can mitigate aspects of inherent risk.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Risk before applying mitigating controls → **inherent risk**.
    

### **21. Risk Mitigation**

- **Concept:** Security controls and monitoring can reduce risk.
    
- **Key distinction:** Increasing security controls can reduce the **likelihood** of an event and therefore reduce its overall risk.
    
- **Exam trigger:** A control reduces the probability of a harmful event → **risk mitigation**.
    

### **22. Residual Risk**

- **Concept:** **Residual risk** is the risk that remains after mitigations have been applied.
    
- **Key distinction:** Mitigation does not necessarily eliminate all risk; the remaining risk is residual risk.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Risk remaining after security controls and other mitigations → **residual risk**.
    

### **23. Overall System Risk Rating**

- **Concept:** The overall risk rating for an AI system is determined by focusing on the **highest residual risk ratings**.
    
- **Key distinction:** The overall rating should account for the most significant remaining risks rather than simply averaging all risks.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Multiple residual risks exist and the question asks for the overall system rating → focus on the **highest residual risks**.
    

### **24. Algorithmic Accountability Act**

- **Concept:** The **Algorithmic Accountability Act** has been introduced multiple times for consideration by the U.S. Congress.
    
- **Key distinction:** The transcript describes it conditionally: **if enacted**, it would require companies to assess the impacts of AI systems they use and sell.
    
- **Exam trigger:** U.S. legislative proposal concerning AI impact assessments and transparency → **Algorithmic Accountability Act**.
    

### **25. Algorithmic Accountability and Transparency**

- **Concept:** The proposed Act would create greater transparency about **when and how AI systems are used**.
    
- **Key distinction:** It is intended to empower consumers to make more informed choices when interacting with AI.
    
- **Exam trigger:** AI legislation focused on impact assessment, transparency, and informed consumer choice → **Algorithmic Accountability Act**.
    

### **26. Algorithmic Fairness and Explainability**

- **Concept:** The transcript describes the Act's goal as protecting Americans from AI systems that could produce **unfair and unexplained results**.
    
- **Example:** Consumers could have a right to understand why a loan application was rejected and whether unfair bias played a role.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Automated decision + unexplained outcome + potential unfair bias → think **algorithmic accountability/transparency**.
    

### **27. Generative AI Transparency**

- **Concept:** Because generative AI models can be complex, it can be difficult to understand how they produce outputs at a deep level.
    
- **Key distinction:** At a minimum, organizations should consider disclosing when users are interacting with **generative AI rather than a human**.
    
- **Exam trigger:** User needs to know whether they are interacting with AI or a human → **AI disclosure/transparency**.
    

### **28. Explainability**

- **Concept:** **Explainability** means understanding how an AI system arrived at an output or inference.
    
- **Key distinction:** Transparency can range from simply disclosing that generative AI is being used to providing deeper explanations of how the model produced an inference.
    
- **Exam trigger:** “Why did the model produce this output?” → **explainability**.
    

### **29. Model-Agnostic Explainability**

- **Concept:** A model-agnostic approach examines a model's **inputs and outputs** without requiring knowledge of its internal structure.
    
- **Key distinction:** The model can be treated as a **black box**, while practitioners determine which features receive the most consideration.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Explainability without depending on the model's internal architecture → **model-agnostic / black-box approach**.
    

### **30. Interpretable Algorithms**

- **Concept:** More interpretable algorithms and techniques can expose aspects of a model's internal workings.
    
- **Examples:** **Decision trees** and **rule-based systems**.
    
- **Key distinction:** These approaches can allow practitioners to observe model weights or understand how the model works internally.
    
- **Exam trigger:** The question emphasizes understanding the model's internal workings → consider **interpretable algorithms**.
    

### **31. Interpretability vs. Performance**

- **Concept:** During early AI/ML development, organizations should consider tradeoffs between **interpretability and performance**.
    
- **Key distinction:** Regulatory requirements should be considered when making this design tradeoff.
    
- **Exam trigger:** A regulated use case requires explainability but the most performant model is less interpretable → consider the **interpretability/performance tradeoff**.
    

### **32. Algorithmic Bias**

- **Concept:** Another major goal of algorithmic accountability is to reduce **bias in decision-making**.
    
- **Key distinction:** The objective is to ensure results are not improperly influenced by personal attributes.
    
- **Exam trigger:** AI decision-making produces potentially unfair outcomes associated with personal attributes → think **algorithmic bias**.
    

### **33. Personal Attributes and Bias**

- **Concept:** The transcript gives examples of potentially sensitive personal attributes including **race, sex, gender identity, religious beliefs, political affiliations, and urban/rural location**.
    
- **Key distinction:** Improper use of such attributes can contribute to unfair decision-making.
    
- **Exam trigger:** A scenario identifies a protected or sensitive characteristic influencing an automated decision → consider **bias risk**.
    

### **34. Bias Testing and Monitoring**

- **Concept:** Bias should be tested and monitored in both **model results and training data**.
    
- **Key distinction:** Checking only the final model output is insufficient; the training data itself can also contain bias.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Question asks where bias should be evaluated → **training data + model results**.
    

### **35. Amazon SageMaker Clarify**

- **Concept:** **Amazon SageMaker Clarify** can help understand how different variables influence model behavior and monitor for **bias and feature-attribution drift**.
    
- **Key distinction:** Clarify supports both understanding variable influence and monitoring specific fairness-related risks.
    
- **Exam trigger:** Bias detection, variable influence, or feature-attribution drift → **SageMaker Clarify**.

# 🧠 Exam Traps

### **1. NIST AI RMF is a law.**

- **Trap:** Treating AI RMF as legally mandatory.
    
- **Correct:** The transcript describes the **NIST AI RMF as voluntary**.
    

### **2. EU AI Act and NIST AI RMF are equivalent.**

- **Trap:** Treating both as the same type of requirement.
    
- **Correct:** The **EU AI Act is a regulation**, while the **NIST AI RMF is a voluntary framework**.
    

### **3. High-risk AI applications are automatically banned.**

- **Trap:** Confusing high-risk with unacceptable-risk applications.
    
- **Correct:** **Unacceptable-risk** applications are banned; **high-risk** applications are subject to specific legal requirements.
    

### **4. Risk is based only on severity.**

- **Trap:** Ignoring likelihood.
    
- **Correct:** The transcript describes risk estimation as **likelihood × severity**.
    

### **5. Residual risk is the risk before controls.**

- **Trap:** Reversing inherent and residual risk.
    
- **Correct:** **Inherent risk** exists before mitigation; **residual risk** remains after mitigation.
    

### **6. Risk mitigation eliminates all risk.**

- **Trap:** Assuming security controls necessarily reduce risk to zero.
    
- **Correct:** Mitigation can reduce risk, but the remaining amount is **residual risk**.
    

### **7. Overall risk should simply average all individual risks.**

- **Trap:** Treating every residual risk equally when determining the overall rating.
    
- **Correct:** The transcript says the overall risk rating focuses on the **highest residual risk ratings**.
    

### **8. Explainability requires access to the model's internal workings.**

- **Trap:** Assuming only interpretable models can be explained.
    
- **Correct:** A **model-agnostic** approach can treat the model as a black box and analyze input/output behavior.
    

### **9. Model-agnostic means the model has no features.**

- **Trap:** Confusing model agnosticism with feature independence.
    
- **Correct:** The approach can determine which **features receive the most consideration** without requiring internal model knowledge.
    

### **10. Explainability and interpretability are identical.**

- **Trap:** Treating all explanation techniques as the same.
    
- **Correct:** The transcript distinguishes black-box/model-agnostic approaches from more **interpretable algorithms** such as decision trees and rule-based systems.
    

### **11. Bias only needs to be tested in model outputs.**

- **Trap:** Ignoring the training data.
    
- **Correct:** Bias should be tested and monitored in **training data as well as model results**.
    

### **12. SageMaker Clarify is only for explainability.**

- **Trap:** Narrowing Clarify to one purpose.
    
- **Correct:** The transcript associates Clarify with understanding variable influence and monitoring **bias and feature-attribution drift**.

An organization is designing an AI system and wants to follow a framework that provides voluntary guidance for trustworthy and responsible AI risk management. The framework defines four functions that structure how an organization addresses AI risks.

Which framework and functions match this requirement?

A. EU AI Act — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage

B. NIST AI RMF — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage

C. ISO 23894 — Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond

D. Algorithmic Accountability Act — Map, Measure, Manage, Explain

**Answer: B — NIST AI RMF — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage**

**Why:** The transcript identifies NIST AI RMF as voluntary and explicitly defines its four functions as **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage**.
